# Prozac — a board script

Seven scenes, written for the authoring tool at `/author`. Paste one scene's
narration into the script bar, press **Narrate**, then draw along with it.

**Lesson title:** `What Prozac actually does`
**Summary:** `Fluoxetine blocks the pump that clears serotonin from the synapse — but the block takes hours and the relief takes weeks, and the gap between those two facts is where most of the popular story about it falls apart.`

Notes on the writing:

- Every scene is 50 to 60 words, which lands between fourteen and eighteen
  seconds of speech — long enough to draw four or five things against.
- No bracketed delivery tags. Those are an ElevenLabs feature; Fish drops them
  silently, so pacing here comes from punctuation and sentence length instead.
- Numbers are spelled out, because the voice reads what it is given.
- The **draw** notes name the phrase to be *hearing* as you draw, not before it.
  The tool stamps whatever is being spoken at that instant as the anchor.

## 1 · The gap

> Two neurons never quite touch. Between them sits a gap about twenty nanometres wide, and one neuron talks to the next by throwing serotonin across it. Then it takes almost all of it back. A pump called the serotonin transporter hoovers the molecules out of the gap and recycles them, in under a second.

*54 words.*

**Draw:**

| when you hear | draw |
| --- | --- |
| "Two neurons never quite touch" | two rounded shapes, left and right, well apart |
| "about twenty nanometres wide" | the gap between them, labelled `20 nm` |
| "throwing serotonin across it" | a scatter of small dots crossing the gap |
| "takes almost all of it back" | a curved arrow sweeping the dots back leftwards |
| "the serotonin transporter" | a small box on the left neuron's edge, labelled `SERT` |

Leave the two neurons and the gap where they are for the next two scenes — this
is the diagram everything else is drawn on top of.

## 2 · The block

> Fluoxetine jams that pump. It sits in the transporter and blocks it, so serotonin that was thrown across the gap stays there instead of being pulled back. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the drug's full name is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor. Selective is the important word.

*51 words.*

| "Selective is the important word" | a ring thrown around `selective` |

## 3 · Why that mattered

> The drugs before it worked too, but they hit everything. Tricyclics blocked histamine, acetylcholine and adrenaline receptors as well, which meant dry mouth, weight gain, a racing heart, and real danger in overdose. Fluoxetine barely touches them. It was not more effective than what came before. It was far easier to live with, and far harder to die from.

*59 words.*

This is the scene that explains why a drug that is *not more effective* became
one of the most prescribed molecules on earth. Give it a comparison layout.

## 4 · The long tail

> One more oddity. Most of these drugs leave the body in a day. Fluoxetine's active breakdown product hangs around for one to two weeks. That is why missing a dose rarely causes the withdrawal that other antidepressants can, and why it takes over a month to fully clear after the last tablet.

*52 words.*

**Draw:**

| when you hear | draw |
| --- | --- |
| "leave the body in a day" | a short bar on a timeline, labelled `most SSRIs · ~1 day` |
| "one to two weeks" | a much longer bar beneath it, `norfluoxetine · 1-2 weeks` |
| "rarely causes the withdrawal" | a green note beside the long bar |
| "over a month to fully clear" | the timeline's far end, marked `~5 weeks` |

## 5 · The puzzle

> Here is the strange part. The pump is blocked within hours of the first dose, but almost nobody feels better for two to six weeks. So whatever is helping, it is not simply more serotonin in the gap. Something slower is happening downstream, and the blocked pump is only what starts it.

*52 words.*

**Draw:**

| when you hear | draw |
| --- | --- |
| "blocked within hours" | a mark at the far left of a timeline, `hours` |
| "two to six weeks" | a mark far to the right, `2-6 weeks` |
| "not simply more serotonin" | the empty stretch between them, in red |
| "Something slower is happening" | a question mark in that gap |

Keep the gap visually *empty*. The point of the scene is that nothing anyone
can point to happens in between, and a crowded middle kills that.

## 6 · What actually changes

> The leading explanation is that the brain adjusts. Serotonin neurons carry sensors on their own bodies that tell them to fire less when levels rise. For the first fortnight those sensors fight the drug. Then they go quiet, firing recovers, and slower changes follow in how neurons grow and connect. The delay is the brain rewiring, not the chemistry.

*59 words.*

## 7 · The story that does not hold

> Which brings us to the thing everyone gets wrong. Because the drug raises serotonin, people concluded depression must be caused by low serotonin. A large review in twenty twenty-two found no consistent evidence for that. Aspirin cures a headache; headaches are not caused by a lack of aspirin. The drug works. The story told about why it works does not hold.

*61 words.*

## The facts behind it

Worth having to hand if you want to change any of the numbers.

- The synaptic cleft is roughly twenty to forty nanometres.
- Fluoxetine was approved by the FDA at the end of 1987 and reached pharmacies
  in 1988. It was not the first SSRI synthesised, but it was the one that
  reached scale.
- Tricyclics are dangerous in overdose mainly through cardiac sodium-channel
  blockade, not through the receptors listed — the receptors are what make them
  unpleasant day to day.
- Fluoxetine's own half-life is one to four days; norfluoxetine, the active
  metabolite, is four to sixteen. Full washout runs to about five weeks.
- The autoreceptors are 5-HT1A, on the cell bodies and dendrites of raphe
  neurons. They desensitise over roughly two to three weeks, which is the
  closest thing to a mechanism for the therapeutic lag.
- The 2022 review is Moncrieff et al., an umbrella review in *Molecular
  Psychiatry*. It found no consistent support for the serotonin-deficiency
  account. It does **not** show that antidepressants are ineffective — that is a
  separate literature, and the large 2018 network meta-analysis by Cipriani et
  al. found all twenty-one studied drugs beat placebo, with modest effect sizes.

Scene 7 is the one to be careful with: it is a claim about a *hypothesis*, not
about whether the drug helps people. The script keeps those apart deliberately.
