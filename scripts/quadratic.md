# Solving a quadratic — two x squared plus five x minus three

Let's solve one properly. Two x squared, plus five x, minus three, equals zero. I'm going to work down the page the way you would in a notebook, one line at a time, and I'll tell you why each line follows from the one above it. By the end you'll have two values of x, and we'll check them both.

First, notice what kind of equation this is. The highest power of x is two, so it's a quadratic. That matters, because a quadratic has a shape: it's a parabola, and a parabola crosses the horizontal axis at most twice. So before doing any arithmetic, I already know I'm looking for at most two answers.

Every quadratic can be written in the same standard form: a x squared, plus b x, plus c, equals zero. Line ours up against it and read off the three numbers. a is two. b is five. And c is minus three — take the sign with the number, that's where most mistakes start.

There's a quantity that decides the whole problem before we solve anything. It's called the discriminant: b squared minus four a c. If it's positive there are two real answers, if it's zero there's exactly one, and if it's negative there are none on the real number line.

So let's compute it. b squared is five squared, which is twenty-five. Four a c is four, times two, times minus three — that's minus twenty-four. Twenty-five minus minus twenty-four is twenty-five plus twenty-four, which is forty-nine. Positive, so we're getting two real answers. And forty-nine is a perfect square, which is a hint this will come out clean.

Now the formula itself. x equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of the discriminant, all over two a. Every quadratic in the world submits to this. Let's substitute what we found: minus five, plus or minus the square root of forty-nine, all over two times two.

The square root of forty-nine is seven. Two times two is four. So the whole thing collapses to: x equals minus five, plus or minus seven, over four. That plus-or-minus is doing real work — it's two calculations pretending to be one, and now we split them.

Take the plus first. Minus five plus seven is two. Two over four is one half. So our first root is x equals one half.

Now the minus. Minus five minus seven is minus twelve. Minus twelve over four is minus three. So the second root is x equals minus three. Two roots, exactly as the discriminant promised.

Never trust an answer you haven't checked. Put one half back into the original: two times one half squared is two times one quarter, which is one half. Five times one half is two and a half. One half plus two and a half is three, minus three is zero. It works.

And check minus three as well. Two times minus three squared is two times nine, which is eighteen. Five times minus three is minus fifteen. Eighteen minus fifteen is three, minus three is zero. Both roots check out.

So there it is. x equals one half, or x equals minus three. Those two numbers are where the parabola crosses the axis, and everything above is just the arithmetic that found them. The method never changes: read off a, b and c, test the discriminant, substitute into the formula, split the plus-or-minus, and check what you get.
